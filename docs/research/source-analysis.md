## 2. Premise and plot arc

### Premise
One ordinary day the world ends: game-style monsters (goblins, giant birds, orcs, later vampires, sea monsters, mutant tigers) appear in cities and civilization collapses within hours. Kim Jae-hyun, 28, is alone in a 30th-floor apartment in Busan. He awakens a single passive skill, **"Ultimate Shut-In's Declaration (Passive) Lv.1"**: nobody may enter his dwelling without his permission. The flip side: he cannot leave it either. Monsters literally cannot cross his threshold, but he has a few days of ramen and bottled water, no power, no water, and his parents are in Seoul, unreachable.

The entire story is the slow inversion of that constraint: a man who cannot step outside turns his apartment into the safest place on Earth, then into a city-state, by growing the "home" definition outward and by sending others out in his place.

### Arc-by-arc summary (novel order; the webtoon follows it closely for the first ~100 episodes)

**Arc 0 – Why he is alone (backstory).** His father bought the apartment with a large loan and the family's savings during a property boom; the market then crashed (the story explicitly references the pandemic era). To qualify for a tax break the family needed someone to live there two years, so Jae-hyun was parked in the flat alone, living off an allowance, with no job or direction. Items in his flat in episode 1 (monitors, mic, clapperboard, wheel controller) hint that he was a small-time streamer or content creator **[inference by fans, uncertain]**.

**Arc 1 – Survival from the window (webtoon eps ~1–5).** Despair, rationing, watching the city burn from the 30th floor. A monster bird (a "Kellican") slams into his window and dies; he receives experience and levels up, which unlocks a **Shop** and a **Warehouse**. He learns he can "register" items he owns so they can be re-bought infinitely at market price, and that killing monsters pays in won. He starts farming goblins by throwing 3 kg dumbbells from the window, builds an improvised flaming spear (kitchen knife + mop handle + cooking oil) to finish a high-level Kellican, and earns his first large sum. Levels restore utilities one by one (electricity, water, gas) through a function called **Dignity Maintenance**.

**Arc 2 – The neighbors (eps ~5–15).** His "dwelling" expands to the whole 30th floor, then the building. He discovers a family next door: Choi Hyeong-jun, wife Park Hye-won, two small daughters. The **Citizen Management** function lets him grant citizenship (protection, shop access) in exchange for quests, and tracks each citizen's trust and loyalty. He faces his first ethical question: the system lets him assign quests, decide resource distribution and even punish failure. Hyeong-jun awakens a strength ability and becomes the **First Vassal**. Other residents of the building are found and recruited: Moon Byeong-ho (teleporter, caring for his grandmother), Kang Deok-su (summons steel-knight armor), Kim Ga-yeong (archer) and her husband Ha Dong-geon (ex-baseball player with a throwing skill), Yoo Hye-rin (poison mist), Kim Geon (bond with a crow). Oh Eon-ju, a mother whose young son Si-woo was killed, is given hope when Jae-hyun discovers a resurrection option; the boy is revived and her loyalty hits 100%.

**Arc 3 – The Haven and the first missions (eps ~15–35).** The building becomes "the Haven," with a ground-floor shop, medical post (Lee Joon-hyuk), solar power amplified by Jae-hyun's three-legged-crow familiar Kkami, training areas and a salary system. Jae-hyun sees everything inside his domain (**Ultimate Shut-In's Eye**) and extends that sight through Seo Ye-jin's tamed mice. Vassal parties led by Ha Dong-geon go out on quests: clearing the block, a subway rescue (families trapped in Busan's metro), a lake mission to recover Ga-yeong's father's body from siren-infested water, a Red Tiger ambush. Reviewers identify the mission starting around webtoon ep 29 as the first real "trial."

**Arc 4 – Territory and factions (eps ~35–70+).** The domain grows from one building to an entire apartment complex and a city block, then annexes subway stations (Seomyeon, Jagalchi, Gyodae, Busan Station) as mostly monster-free underground corridors. The team fights goblin hordes, black orcs, marine monsters, a tree monster, a cyclops (one-shot by Kkami's giant form), and **vampires**: former humans in a Lesser/Lower/Intermediate/Higher hierarchy who form their own factions in apartment blocks (e.g., the Blue Castle complex in Dae-yeon, cleared from a vampire leader). Light attacks, poison and dark flame counter their regeneration. Some readers criticized that the cast kills vampires who still look and think like people without much moral debate.

**Arc 5 – Toward the family and beyond (novel mid-to-late).** Jae-hyun's goal is to reach his parents in Seoul. The novel grants "Kin Powers": once family is reunited they receive ruler-type titles and can awaken others, so the family becomes a network of nodes. The scale balloons: district → city → national level, modern infrastructure (power grid, water, comms) rebuilt inside the domain, other survivor groups and even a Japanese survivor (Takaki Minakuchi, Nagasaki) hinting at international scope, non-human races (elves, a "World Tree" storyline) near the end. Korean reviewers consistently say the first half is strong and the last ~60 chapters are rushed, conflicts resolve too easily, and the ending feels abrupt for a 258-chapter novel. The webtoon has not reached these arcs.

## 3. Main character: Kim Jae-hyun (김재현; "Jaehyeon" in the EN webtoon)

- **Starting state.** 28, unemployed, living alone on his parents' money in a flat he did not choose, vaguely ashamed of being a burden. Not a classic hikikomori (he is not socially phobic), but aimless and inert; the apocalypse then makes the inertia literal. Early chapters show self-pity, panic about food, and guilt about having ignored his parents.
- **Personality.** Calm, analytical, a tinkerer: he reads his skill like a rulebook and looks for loopholes (the window is "inside," so throwing things out counts as hunting; registering a half-empty ketchup bottle and buying it back new). Pragmatic to the point of coldness in some choices (he announces that only those who prove useful get supplies), but reviewers agree he is never cruel and bends the system to protect people. Grows into a charismatic, humble commander who delegates and whose vassals genuinely love him.
- **Motivations.** (1) survive, (2) reach and protect his parents in Seoul, (3) give the people under his roof a future, (4) understand what caused the apocalypse and his power.
- **Arc.** From burden to provider; from "can't leave" as a curse to "doesn't need to leave" as a strength; from controlling resources to building institutions (salaries, roles, a medical center, a shop). The tagline of the webtoon is literally about saving neighbors from your couch, and the emotional core is that his confinement forces him to work *through* other people, so his growth is measured by their growth.
- **Flaws reviewers note.** He is overpowered early; obstacles tend to be solved by a new system function appearing at the right time; he "abuses" his authority and everyone cheerfully goes along.

## 5. World, systems and rules

### The apocalypse
No tutorial, no dungeon gates: monsters simply appear in the real world one day and keep evolving (goblins → orcs → vampires → giants). Physics stays normal; most survivors have no powers. Awakening is tied to Jae-hyun's domain: citizens who earn enough trust can be "registered" as vassals and receive a power, so *he* is the source of superhumans. The origin of the event and of his power is a background mystery.

### The core skill: Ultimate Shut-In (집구석 절대자)
- **Declaration (passive).** Defines a "homebound area" (dwelling). Nothing enters without permission; he cannot exit. Permission is explicit and revocable.
- **Levels.** XP comes from monsters killed by him *or by his citizens* (he gets a cut), and from quests. Each level unlocks a new function or expands an old one: more floor space, utilities, bigger shop, higher citizen cap (starts at 600), more vassal slots.
- **Expansion.** The "dwelling" grows in rings: flat → floor → building → complex → block → connected subway stations → district. Expansion happens via level-ups and by clearing/claiming adjacent space (annexes, outposts).

### Sub-functions (system windows)
- **Shop.** Supernatural store. Objects he owns can be *registered* and then bought back infinitely at normal market price; registering a damaged item and buying it returns a new one ("restoration"). Later a physical shop on the ground floor sells registered goods to citizens, which is the main incentive for them to earn money by hunting.
- **Warehouse.** Infinite inventory; items can be summoned anywhere in the domain (he drops dumbbells on goblins from storage).
- **Wallet / settlement money.** Monster kills pay in won; he funds salaries (employee 100K/day up to president 300K/day in the novel's table) and purchases.
- **Eye.** Omniscient view of everything in the domain, multiple viewpoints at once; extended by Ye-jin's mice as roaming cameras.
- **Dignity Maintenance.** Restores power, water, gas inside the domain; later upgrades allow granting powers to citizens (vassal registration) and trust-based rewards.
- **Citizen Management.** Grant/revoke citizenship; see trust and loyalty percentages; assign quests with rewards and penalties; distribute XP/money; assign roles (jobs). Vassals are citizens who crossed trust/loyalty thresholds; they get titles (First Vassal, Knight, Spearman, Tamer…) that multiply XP and money and boost stats proportionally to loyalty. "Vassal Summon" teleports a vassal to him.
- **Health / Serene Mind.** Passive immunity; clear thinking under pain; hints of eventual immortality.
- **Invisible Hand.** Telekinesis inside the domain.
- **Familiars.** Kkami (sun crow) adds an energy-economy layer: sunlight → power → income.
- **Kin Powers.** Family members gain ruler titles and can awaken others (novel late game).
- **Resurrection.** An expensive, rare function used on Si-woo.

### Progression structure
1. Personal loop: farm monsters from the window → level → unlock utilities/shop → survive better.
2. Social loop: find survivors → citizenship → quests → trust/loyalty → vassal → new powers → bigger hunts → more money/XP for him → larger domain → more survivors.
3. Territory loop: clear adjacent area → annex → outpost → corridor (subway) → next cluster of survivors.
4. Narrative goal: reach the family; late game, rebuild infrastructure and confront larger factions.

### What "shut-in" means mechanically and thematically
Mechanically: the protagonist is a *stationary* commander; all agency goes through delegation, surveillance, logistics and base-building (reviewers compare it to tower defense plus kingdom builder). Thematically: a person who had withdrawn from life becomes the center of a community precisely by making his home a public good; the walls that trap him are the walls that protect everyone. The irony is that he never "leaves the shut-in life" physically; he leaves it socially and morally, by taking responsibility for others. For our game, the inversion is the point: the *player* is the one who must go out, and the game's "Absolute" is the home base that grows when they do.

## 6. Themes and tone

- **Isolation turned into responsibility.** Confinement is a curse that becomes leadership.
- **Family and neighbors.** The emotional beats are almost all family: the Choi daughters, Eon-ju and Si-woo, Ga-yeong's father, Byeong-ho's grandmother, Jae-hyun's parents. Reviewers single out these moments as the best in the series.
- **Power and ethics.** Absolute authority over who eats and who lives; the story repeatedly asks whether a benevolent dictator is still a dictator, then mostly answers "it's fine because he is nice," which critics flag as its weakness.
- **Modern-infrastructure fantasy.** Instead of medieval kingdom building, it is about getting the tap water back on, solar panels, subway corridors, salaries, a convenience store. Very Korean-apartment-life texture (property loans, residency tax rules, instant noodles, bottled water brands).
- **Tone.** Light, fast-paced wish-fulfillment with real tension only in the first ~35 chapters; humor comes from loophole-abuse and from the pun of the title; the webtoon is more earnest than comedic. Low gore, all-ages rating.
- **Power fantasy, deliberately low-stakes.** Readers describe it as comfortable: the home base cannot fall, so the drama lives in the people going out.

## 7. Visual style and iconic imagery (for asset generation)

Observed from the official WEBTOON episode 1 and reviews (art: Bihyeon for eps 1–40; Tanto afterward, with readers noting a flatter, more generic look after the switch):

- **Opening palette.** Night cityscape of Busan high-rise apartment towers in flames: near-black blues and greys with saturated orange/yellow fire, floating embers, a cracked-glass overlay over the skyline, large distressed sound-effect lettering. Caption boxes in white on black with red splatter.
- **The apartment interior.** A modern, slightly messy bachelor flat: PC desk with three monitors, streaming mic, game controllers, instant-noodle stockpile, bottled water packs, a wide living-room window that becomes a "hunting hide." Daylight scenes are clean, pale, slightly 3D-modeled backgrounds (reviewers mention 3D backgrounds), with the flat contrasting the chaos outside.
- **System UI.** Translucent blue-white rectangular windows with a thin border, bold header (skill name, level, "Passive"), bracketed messages; shop/warehouse grids; percentage bars for trust/loyalty. Typical Korean "system" manhwa style.
- **Monsters.** Classic green goblins with ragged loincloths, bulky orcs with tusks, the Kellican (giant dark bird with hooked beak), red-furred tiger with jagged dark stripes, pale blood-streaked vampires with blood tendrils, sirens (fish-human), land sharks bursting from the ground, sky marlins, a tree monster, a cyclops.
- **Heroes' kit.** Improvised weapons (dumbbells, mop-handle spear, cooking oil fire), then a mix of modern gear (baseball-pitcher throw, bows, guns) and summoned fantasy armor (steel knight with halberd); a small black three-legged crow that becomes a radiant giant bird; swarms of mice as scouts.
- **Protagonist look.** Late-20s Korean man, black hair, black eyes, hoodie/sweats at home; calm expression; often framed looking down from the window or at floating system windows.
- **Iconic images.** The lit window on a dark tower; dumbbells raining on goblins; a map of the city with a glowing, growing domain boundary; the ground-floor convenience shop inside a fortress; a crow sunbathing on a solar panel.

## 9. Game design hooks (mobile PWA, real-life activity → in-game progress)

Design premise: invert the source. In the story the hero cannot leave and others go out for him. In our game **the player is both the Absolute (the home base) and the vassal (the one who goes out)**. Every real-life action is a "quest completed outside the domain" that feeds XP, money and loyalty back into a home that grows on a map. Nothing requires the fiction's names.

1. **The Domain ring map.** The home base is a single room on a stylized block map. Cumulative "outside minutes" (GPS optional; manual check-in by default) expand the domain in rings: room → flat → floor → building → block → district. Each ring unlocks new facilities and new quest types. Visual payoff: a glowing boundary creeping over a city map.
2. **Two-sided avatar.** A sedentary "Absolute" avatar sits at the desk; a "Vassal" avatar is the player's field self. Going outside switches the UI to vassal mode (quest log, timer, return-home button). Returning home triggers a "settlement" screen: XP, money, loot, loyalty gained.
3. **Habit = vassal.** Each habit category (Move, Outside, Clean, Social, Study, Sleep, Eat well, Hygiene) is a named vassal with its own title, level and loyalty bar. Loyalty rises with completed quests and decays slowly when neglected; a vassal with 0% loyalty "leaves the Haven" and must be re-recruited (a soft, non-punishing streak reset).
4. **Daily quest board issued by the system.** 3–5 quests generated each morning from the player's chosen habits and difficulty (e.g., 20 minutes outdoors in daylight, 10-minute tidy, message one friend, lights out by 23:30). Quests pay settlement money + XP; a "bonus contract" doubles rewards for the hardest one. Weekly "missions" are multi-day chains (subway-rescue-style).
5. **Dignity Maintenance = utilities meter.** Power, water and gas in the Haven are tied to sleep, hygiene and meals respectively. Miss sleep → the lights flicker, the base dims; skip meals → water pressure drops. The base never falls (as in the source) but it degrades visibly; restoring a utility is a satisfying small loop.
6. **Shop registration (photo-free).** The player "registers" real items they want to make habitual (running shoes, a water bottle, a study book, a vacuum cleaner) as named inventory entries. Using them in a quest "buys them back" infinitely and builds an item mastery meter; mastered items become cosmetic decor in the base.
7. **Sun familiar.** A small crow familiar charges only when the player logs daylight outdoor time (optionally reads the device light sensor). Its charge powers the base's solar grid and pays passive income overnight; at full charge it can "one-shot" a weekly boss. Direct mechanical nudge to get sunlight.
8. **Monster waves as weekly review.** Every Sunday a wave attacks the domain. Defense strength is the sum of the week's habit levels; the player watches the wave resolve as an auto-battle. Winning expands the ring; losing only delays expansion (no base loss), consistent with the source's "home can't fall" rule.
9. **Citizens as needs.** Unlock NPC citizens (a family next door, a grandmother, a doctor) who move into the base as rings grow. Each citizen "needs" a habit (the kids need a cleaned common room, the doctor needs sleep logs) and in return produces resources or buffs. Population cap grows with consistency, not intensity, rewarding long streaks over binges.
10. **Trust vs loyalty split.** Trust = how many days this week the habit was attempted; loyalty = how long the streak has lasted overall. Showing both prevents the "one missed day ruins everything" feeling and mirrors the source's two metrics.
11. **Resurrection token.** Settlement money can buy one "revive" per month that restores a lost streak (Si-woo beat). Rare, expensive, emotionally framed.
12. **Scouting mice = planning.** At night the player sends "mice" to scout tomorrow: picking tomorrow's quests in advance gives a 10% reward bonus. Encourages intention-setting without being another to-do app.
13. **Titles as achievements with mechanical teeth.** First Vassal (first 7-day streak), Knight (30 days), Spearman (100 outdoor sessions), Tamer (10 social quests), Facility Manager (cleaning), each granting a permanent multiplier to one habit's rewards, exactly like the source's titles.
14. **Improvised weapons = combo quests.** Completing two habits in one outing (walk + call a friend; gym + grocery) forges an "improvised weapon" item with a silly name and a temporary damage buff in the weekly wave. Encourages habit stacking.
15. **Corridors and outposts for recurring places.** The player can mark real places (gym, library, park, a friend's home) as "outposts"; visiting them N times turns them into "subway corridors" that make related quests cheaper. All stored locally; no server required for a PWA.
16. **System-window notifications.** Push notifications styled as system windows (level up, quest issued, utility restored). Offline-first with a service worker; quests can be logged offline and settled when back online.
17. **The ethics knob.** A "benevolence" setting that decides what happens to a neglected citizen: in Benevolent mode they just wait; in Strict mode they leave. Lets players choose their own pressure level, nodding to the source's debate about the hero's authority.
18. **Episodic story in system windows.** Each ring unlock plays a short original vignette (text + a few illustrations) in the same confined-commander spirit: a new neighbor, a rescue, a shop opening. Keeps it a game, not a tracker.
19. **Risks to design around.** Self-report honesty (keep rewards low-stakes and cosmetic-heavy; no leaderboards early); GPS/privacy (opt-in, on-device only); over-gamification causing anxiety (no base loss, generous catch-up, loyalty decay slow); shame spirals for people with real hikikomori tendencies (language stays warm, "the home is always safe"); feature creep (ship the ring map, quests, utilities and the weekly wave first); and IP (section 10).

## 10. IP / legal note (fan-inspired game on GitHub)

**Safe to use (not protectable or generic):**
- The abstract premise and mechanics: a home-bound character whose domain grows; citizens/vassals with trust and loyalty; quests, XP, a shop that re-sells registered items, utilities restored by levels, monster waves. Game mechanics and ideas are not copyrightable; many of these tropes predate this work (system/level manhwa, tower defense, base builders).
- Themes: isolation turned into responsibility, family, rebuilding infrastructure, "leaving the shut-in life."
